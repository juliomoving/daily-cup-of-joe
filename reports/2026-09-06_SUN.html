IF THIS RHYMES WITH 2000
Nasdaq fell -78.0% peak→trough (2000-03-10 → 2002-10-09, ~31 months); it took ~15 years to reclaim the high.

The honest read is that this is a different bubble, not a smaller one. In 2000 the excess was in price — 175x trailing earnings on an index, a 50% stretch above trend, and a public that had lost its mind. Today's excess is in risk pricing: an index at 37.45x is expensive but explicable, while IG spreads at 133% of 2000-peak tightness, Baa spreads at 155%, VIX complacency at 200% and financial conditions at 163% of 2000's looseness are not explicable at all. Investors have not paid a crazy price for equities; they have accepted a crazy price for bearing risk. That matters for how it ends. A price bubble deflates when the marginal buyer runs out — a slow, grinding 31-month affair like 2000–2002. A risk-pricing bubble deflates when the marginal lender changes their mind, which is fast and correlated. The two live indicators to watch are the SOX multiple at 53.42x, already 84% of the way to 2000 conditions and the place where a price bubble would actually be forming, and margin debt at 45.6% annual growth, which is the leverage that converts a spread move into a forced-selling move. The strongest counterargument is real: both ISM surveys are expanding at 54.6 and 55.4 with essentially no rollover signal, unemployment is 4.1%, and the yield curve is positively sloped at 41.6bp. Bubbles do not usually burst into an expanding economy — they need a catalyst. In 2000 it was the Fed and the capex cliff. Today the candidate catalyst is not the Fed at all; it is $96.51 Brent with the Strait of Hormuz closed, feeding a flexible CPI already running at 4.68% into a Fed chair who has told you plainly that he does not think inflation's underlying trend has improved. That is the sequence to fear: oil forces the inflation hand, the long end at 5.2423% keeps rising, and credit at the 4th percentile discovers it has no cushion.
