The story of the session is a textbook bear steepener colliding with an energy shock. The 2-year fell 1.45bp to 4.2726% while the 10-year added 7.11bp to 4.6773% and the 30-year added 11.21bp to 5.2007% — the long end repricing inflation and term premium, not growth. The trigger was crude: Brent +7.68% to 90.55 and WTI +6.56% (+$5.20) at 84.46, with the Iran conflict re-escalating overnight. Equities took it badly and breadth was ugly: the Dow -2.19% (-1,153.18 to 51,594.14), Nasdaq Composite -1.74% (-433.97 to 24,442.94), S&P 500 -1.52% (-112.63 to 7,316.15), Russell 2000 -1.61% to 2,906.31, VIX +13.45% to 20.66. Europe was cushioned (STOXX 600 -0.29%, Euro Stoxx 50 -0.65%) and the FTSE 100 actually rose 0.34% on its energy weighting, while the Nikkei fell 1.49%. The tell that this is a US-specific risk-premium event, not a global flight to quality, is the dollar: DXY fell 0.575 to 100.842 (-0.57%) into higher US yields — the currency did not get paid for the yield move. Gold barely budged (+0.02% to 4,068.22) and silver slipped 0.14%, so this was not a classic panic hedge bid either. Credit is the great dissenter: US IG OAS unchanged at 0.80%, US HY 2.82% (+0.01), EUR HY 2.91% (+0.01), EM USD 1.68% (+0.02) — all still sitting in the tightest tenth of their own histories. Sentiment: risk-off in equities, deeply complacent in credit, and increasingly suspicious of the long end of the curve. Versus the prior session, this is the first day the market has priced the oil move as an inflation problem rather than an energy-earnings story.

💱FX Analysis

The FX tape is the most informative thing on the screen tonight. The DXY fell 0.575 to 100.842 (-0.57%) on a day when the US 10-year rose 7.11bp and the 30-year rose 11.21bp. Normally higher US yields pull capital in and lift the dollar; when the dollar falls into a long-end selloff, the market is charging the US a risk premium rather than rewarding it with a yield premium. The majors themselves barely moved: EUR/USD 1.1464 (-0.03%), GBP/USD 1.3366 (-0.03%), USD/CHF 0.8139 (-0.02%), USD/JPY 163.40 (essentially flat, -0.01 net) despite JGB yields falling 1.7bp — carry is doing the work there, not rate differentials. USD/CNH was unchanged at 6.7605, a deliberate stillness, and USD/BRL printed 5.1186. So the index decline is broad and quiet rather than driven by any one cross — the hallmark of reserve-manager style diversification out of dollars, not speculative positioning. For a commodity-importing world this softer dollar slightly cushions the oil shock; for the US it compounds it, because a weaker dollar plus $90 Brent is imported inflation on top of energy inflation.

US HY OAS edged up just 0.01 to 2.82%, z-score -0.84, 9th percentile (range 2.3% to 19.7%). Europe is the same picture: EUR IG 0.79% (z -0.86, 8th percentile), EUR HY 2.91% (z -0.69, 11th percentile). EM USD is the most stretched of all at 1.68% (z -1.31, 5th percentile). Against every crisis benchmark — GFC 2008, the 2011 euro debt crisis, COVID 2020, the 2022 hiking shock — today's spreads register at the 0th percentile: we are nowhere near stress conditions. Key levels and what it would take to reach them. For US HY, 3.25% is the first line that would signal the equity selloff had become a funding problem; that needs a sustained oil-driven margin squeeze plus one or two high-profile leveraged-issuer disappointments — think two consecutive weeks of $90+ Brent with weak consumer guidance. HY at 4.00% would require an actual default-cycle repricing, historically accompanied by IG through 1.20%. For IG, 0.95%-1.00% is the level that would confirm the long-end move is contaminating corporate funding; the mechanism would be issuers pulling deals as the 30-year holds above 5.20% and all-in coupons become prohibitive. Until IG breaks 0.90%, treat equity weakness as a valuation and rate-discount event, not a credit event. The risk in the other direction is that spreads this tight offer almost no cushion — from the 5th percentile, the asymmetry of being long credit is as poor as it gets.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 72/100 — Sticky, broad, and now getting an energy tailwind — the least helpful combination. The headline core measures look almost benign (Core CPI 2.6% year-over-year, Core PCE 3.41%), but the underlying-trend gauges all cluster in the 56th-76th percentile of their own histories: Median CPI 2.71%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.63%, Median PCE 2.82%, trimmed-mean PCE 2.41% and core sticky CPI 2.81%. None is falling meaningfully; trimmed-mean PCE is actually rising. The breadth data is the real problem. On the Dallas Fed distribution, 14.0% of the PCE basket is inflating faster than 10%, another 10.0% at 5-10%, and 34.0% at 3-5% — that is 58% of consumer spending rising at 3% or more, against only 14.8% running below 2% and 27.2% outright falling. Inflation this broad is not a relative-price story that averages out; it is a level shift that requires either weaker demand or tighter policy to break. Layer on Brent at 90.55 (+7.68%) and flexible CPI already at 5.06% and rising, and the near-term path for headline is up. The offset is that markets do not believe it: 5-year breakeven inflation of 2.27% and 1-year of 1.83% say investors expect this to fade, and wage pressure is contained (average hourly earnings 3.5%, ECI 0.9%). That gap between what the basket is doing and what the bond market has priced is the trade — and it is why the 30-year sold off 11.21bp today.

🏦Fed Chair Warsh
NEAR TERM

Hold, with a hawkish framing — no cut at the next meeting. This is an inference from his on-record positions, not from any guidance he has given. At the ECB Forum on Central Banking in early July he explicitly declined to signal what the Fed would do at the meeting later that month, but did say inflation was too elevated (CNBC). On July 14 he pledged a policy 'regime change' to rid the American people of the inflation 'tax' (CNBC). A chair who frames inflation as a tax on citizens, with Core PCE at 3.41% year-over-year, 58% of the PCE basket inflating at 3% or faster, and Brent up 7.68% today, has no rhetorical path to easing at the next meeting. Flag: this is my inference from his stated views — I have no reporting on his current vote intention.

~1 YEAR

Cautious, back-loaded easing at best, with the bigger changes being institutional rather than the level of rates. Over roughly a year I would expect at most modest cuts, and only after core measures show sustained downward movement rather than the flat 2.4-2.8% cluster we have now — and I would put more weight on framework and communications reform than on the funds rate. He has criticised Fed communications as a source of policy error that placed the central bank too close to the centre of market decision-making, noted at his April confirmation hearing that Fed officials 'speak quite frequently', and CNBC has characterised his approach as one where 'silence may be the point' (CNBC). He has also called the 2020 flexible average inflation targeting framework 'a mistake' (CNBC) and told his first congressional hearing that in six weeks he had caused 'a sea change in new thinking, the beginning of a set of reforms that are going to be put in place across at least five dimensions in monetary policy', with a plan to establish five task forces (The Hill; CNN). Inference: expect a tighter, more hawkish target framework and materially fewer speeches — which mechanically raises the market impact of each FOMC statement and of the data itself.

The practical trading consequence of a Warsh Fed is that the market has less policy information to trade on and therefore prices more risk premium into the long end — which is exactly the shape of today's move, with the 2-year down 1.45bp and the 30-year up 11.21bp. A chair who deliberately reduces forward guidance removes the anchor that used to cap term premium; the curve at +40.47bp of 2s10s slope and a 5.2007% 30-year is what that looks like in practice. He also cited the benefits of the AI investment boom alongside his inflation pledge (CNBC), which cuts against any hope that he treats a tech-led equity de-rating as a reason to ease — he is more likely to read the AI capex cycle as a supply-side positive that lets him stay tight for longer. The asymmetry for investors: front-end rates are anchored by a chair who will not cut into 3.41% Core PCE, while the long end has no guidance backstop. That argues for the steepener, not for duration. All directional calls in this section are my inference from his public record; none of it is reported guidance.
