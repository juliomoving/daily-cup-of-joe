The war at sea became a market event today. CENTCOM's destruction of five Iranian crude carriers on September 8 and Tehran's threat to declare a restricted maritime zone through the Strait of Hormuz pushed Brent up 3.83% to 101.67 and WTI up 3.25% to 96.05, and every other asset class traded off that single input. Equities sold off across the board: the S&P 500 closed 7636.36, down 0.48% (-37.16), the Nasdaq Composite 26253.34, down 0.64%, the Dow 52380.66, down 0.77% (-405.41), and the Russell 2000 2921.234, down 1.32% - the breadth signal that matters, because the domestic small-cap index is where an oil-cost shock and a higher discount rate both land. VIX rose 4.71% to 16.46, up but hardly panicked. Europe took the harder hit given its energy import bill: Euro Stoxx 50 -1.58%, STOXX 600 -1.41%, FTSE 100 -1.31%, while the Nikkei held up at -0.19%. Sovereign bonds sold off in sympathy with the inflation impulse - US 10Y +5.24bp to 4.8406%, 2Y +3.59bp to 4.4295%, 30Y +4.35bp to 5.2889%, with the Bund +7.8bp to 3.444% and the 10Y Gilt +8.8bp to 5.261% leading, and only the JGB lower at 2.886%. That is the tell: this was not a growth scare, because a growth scare rallies bonds. This was an inflation scare layered onto a Fed that markets increasingly think will hike on September 16. Credit did not blink - US IG option-adjusted spread unchanged at 0.80% and US HY unchanged at 2.67%, both sitting in the bottom few percent of their own histories. FX was inert, with the dollar index at 98.773, essentially flat, which tells you positioning has not yet capitulated in either direction. Versus the prior session the character of the tape changed from grinding drift to a coordinated energy-led repricing, and sentiment is best described as uneasy rather than fearful: investors sold the cyclical and rate-sensitive parts of the market, bought energy, and left credit and the dollar untouched, which is what a market does when it suspects a problem but has not yet priced one.

There is no credit stress priced anywhere. Key levels and the conditions to reach them. For US IG, the first meaningful warning is 1.00%, twenty basis points of widening; that requires either a hawkish surprise at the September 16 FOMC that shifts the terminal rate materially higher, or a sustained Brent price above $110 that starts compressing corporate margins in transport, chemicals and consumer sectors. The second level, 1.22%, is where the computed panel places the 2000-peak comparison for credit tightness, and reaching it would require an actual default impulse rather than a repricing. For US HY, 3.00% is the line that would signal the marginal borrower is being cut off - only 33 basis points away, and the tech-bubble panel already flags a comparison value there. For EM USD at 1.56%, the risk is asymmetric and specific: a genuine closure of the Strait of Hormuz would hit energy-importing emerging sovereigns through both the current account and the fiscal channel, and a move to 2.50% would be fast rather than gradual from a percentile rank of 2. The honest reading is this - credit is priced for a world in which nothing goes wrong, and the compensation for being wrong is the thinnest it has been in decades. That is not a forecast of imminent widening. It is an observation that the option to hedge is currently cheap and the payoff to being early is small while the cost of being late is large.

The conflict has moved decisively into the sea lanes, and that is why it is now a markets story rather than a headlines story. On September 5 the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer; both evaded, and there were no US casualties. CENTCOM responded by permanently disabling the Iranian tankers Downy and Stark 1 and destroying the Kylo. On September 8, CENTCOM destroyed five Iranian crude carriers in what is described as the largest single day of tanker destruction of the war, and the IRGC responded with 20 ballistic missiles at the Al Azraq air base in Jordan; Jordan's armed forces stated they intercepted 18, with two falling in unpopulated areas and no casualties. The market-relevant development is what Iran said on September 7. Separately, reporting indicates Russia is aiding Iran with the development of supersonic cruise missiles, with the aim of giving Iran the capability to strike and neutralise US aircraft carriers in the region - a capability change that, if realised, alters the risk calculus for carrier presence in the Gulf and therefore for the security of the shipping lanes. The market is currently pricing a disruption, not a closure. But the asymmetry is severe, and with VIX at 16.46 and credit spreads unchanged, the market is not paying much for that tail.
