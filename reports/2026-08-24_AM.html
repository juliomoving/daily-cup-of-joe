IF THIS RHYMES WITH 2000
Nasdaq fell -78.0% peak→trough (2000-03-10 → 2002-10-09, ~31 months); it took ~15 years to reclaim the high.

The useful thing about the 2000 analogue is not the top, it is the aftermath: the Nasdaq fell 78.0% from 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 to 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002, and it took 31 months to do it (computed). That is the shape of the risk — a long grinding derating, not a crash. Which is why the anchored-path comparison matters more than any single valuation number. Anchored at the October 1990 bear-market bottom, the dot-com path was at 100 in month 0 and peaked at 1,423.9 in month 113 — a fourteen-bagger over nine and a half years. The current cycle, anchored at October 2022, sits at month 46 with a reading of 238.3, having peaked at 245.5 in month 43. At the equivalent month 46 the dot-com path was at 232.1. Those are almost identical — this cycle is tracking the 1990s cycle with uncanny precision, and it is roughly 5.5 years short of where that path topped out. Read against the 1994 pre-internet reset anchor, the dot-com path was at 264.7 by month 46 versus today's 238.3, so on that framing today is running slightly behind. Neither anchoring supports the claim that we are at the top. Both support the claim that we are unmistakably inside the same kind of episode. What is genuinely different, and worse: credit. In 2000 the excess was concentrated in equity multiples and the credit market was already pricing risk — today IG and Baa spreads read 133% and 151% of the 2000 condition, meaning bondholders are demanding less compensation now than they did at the peak of a mania. That is the pipe through which an AI capex disappointment would transmit into the real economy, because unlike 1999's dot-coms, today's leaders are funding datacentres with investment-grade debt. What is genuinely better: there is no retail mania. AAII net sentiment is -4.4, consumer confidence is 90.8 against 140.8 in 2000, the IPO window is closed, and the Nasdaq is 8% above trend rather than 50%. Bubbles do not usually end while the public is grumpy. The practical synthesis is that this looks like 1998, not March 2000 — expensive, concentrated, credit-complacent, and with the melt-up phase still ahead rather than behind. That framing argues for staying long with hedges bought at a VIX of 15.92 rather than for selling, while treating any move in HY through 3.25% or a Nasdaq death cross (which the computed data says reversed 60% of the time on 10 observations, so wait for confirmation) as the signal that the regime has actually changed.
