The single biggest story was crude: WTI fell 5.69% to 75.77 and Brent fell 5.90% to 78.83 as Washington paused the threatened air campaign against Iran and claimed talks. That one move reset the whole macro chessboard — cheaper energy means a lower inflation tax on the consumer, a lower terminal-rate path, and a higher multiple for long-duration equities. The result was a broad melt-up versus the prior session: S&P 500 +1.79% to 7,736.52 (+136.02), Nasdaq Composite +2.59% to 26,584.99 (+671.09), Dow +1.71% to 54,085.88, Russell 2000 +1.85% to 3,036.98. Europe followed with less enthusiasm (Euro Stoxx 50 +0.94%, STOXX 600 +0.73%, FTSE 100 +0.20%) and Japan barely participated (Nikkei +0.32%). Treasuries rallied in sympathy: 2Y -4.6bp to 4.1917%, 10Y -6.3bp to 4.6126%, 30Y -5.3bp to 5.1743%; Bunds -4.5bp to 3.107% and Gilts -5.6bp to 4.897%. JGBs were the lone rate market to sell off (+2.5bp to 2.86%). FX was inert — DXY 99.883 (-0.01%), EUR/USD 1.1530, USD/JPY 157.73 — which tells you this was a growth/energy trade, not a dollar trade. Sentiment: greedy, but not blindly so. VIX rose 4.04% to 16.50 even as the index gained nearly 2%, and the AAII retail survey is net -11.1 bearish — a rally the public still distrusts. Credit shrugged: US IG OAS 0.77% and US HY OAS 2.71%, both within a hair of the tightest levels in their history.

“When bombs get postponed, barrels get cheaper and multiples get bigger — enjoy it, but don't marry it.”
📊Markets

Every major equity index closed green, and the dispersion tells the story. US large-cap growth did the heavy lifting — Nasdaq's +2.59% versus the Dow's +1.71% is a 88bp growth-over-value gap in a single session — while the Russell 2000's +1.85% shows the bid was broad rather than purely mega-cap. That combination (falling yields, falling oil, small caps participating) is the textbook signature of a disinflationary-growth impulse rather than a defensive flight. Europe's more muted +0.73% to +0.94% and the FTSE's +0.20% reflect their heavier energy and resources weightings: a 6% crude drop is a tax cut for US consumers and a hit to UK index earnings. Japan's +0.32% was capped by the only rate market that sold off, with the 10Y JGB up 2.5bp to 2.86%, keeping domestic duration a headwind. The bond rally was orderly and roughly parallel — 2s down 4.6bp, 10s down 6.3bp, 30s down 5.3bp — leaving 2s10s at a computed 42.09bps, still positively sloped and modestly steeper. That is not a market pricing a growth scare; it is a market pricing less imported inflation. The one nagging detail: VIX up 4.04% to 16.50 on a 1.8% up day. Vol rising with price usually means the rally was bought with upside calls and hedged, not chased with naked length — or that someone is paying up for protection into next week's CPI. Copper +1.57% and silver +0.03% corroborate the growth read; gold flat at 4,077.86 says nobody added crisis insurance today.

🔄Sector Analysis

This was a growth-and-cyclicals day with a defensive give-back. In the US, Info Tech +4.09% on the day dwarfed everything, followed by Materials +1.99%, Industrials +1.80% and Financials +0.88%. The losers were the bond-proxy and defensive complex: Utilities -0.60%, Energy -0.49% (the obvious casualty of a 6% crude drop), Consumer Discretionary -0.48%, Health Care -0.14% and Real Estate -0.13%. Global sectors tracked the US almost exactly — Info Tech +3.29%, Materials +2.10%, Industrials +1.68%, with Energy -1.06% and Utilities -0.42% — so this was a worldwide factor move, not a US idiosyncrasy. Step back to the one-week window and the rotation is even clearer: US Discretionary +8.79%, Info Tech +7.95% and Comm Services +7.16% versus Utilities -3.11%, Health Care -3.09% and Cons Staples -2.24%. That is an eight-to-eleven-point weekly spread between growth cyclicals and defensives — a violent, one-directional risk-on rotation. The one-month window complicates the picture: Energy still leads at +10.57% (US) and +9.94% (global), a legacy of the pre-de-escalation war premium that today's crude collapse begins to unwind, while Utilities -2.66% and Materials -0.72% lag. Year-to-date leadership remains Energy +27.83%, Info Tech +21.91% and Industrials +17.87% against Utilities +2.01%, Cons Discretionary +3.04% and Health Care +4.08%. The tension worth watching: Energy is the top YTD US sector but was down today and is now facing a structurally lower oil price, while global Cons Discretionary is actually negative YTD at -1.02% despite a +6.98% week — an enormous divergence between US and global consumer strength that argues against extrapolating today's US-centric melt-up worldwide.

Europe echoes it: EUR IG 0.78% (z -0.88, rank 7), EUR HY 2.90% (z -0.70, rank 11). The most extreme reading is EM USD at 1.66% with a z-score of -1.33 and a rank of 4 — emerging-market dollar borrowers are being charged less relative to history than any other cohort here, which is precisely what you'd expect after a 6% oil drop and a flat dollar, and precisely what unwinds fastest if either reverses. Day-over-day the direction was mildly mixed: US IG -1bp and US HY -8bp tighter, but EUR HY +1bp and EM USD +2bp wider, a small crack worth noting. Key levels and what it takes to reach them. For IG: 71bp is the historical floor — reaching it needs continued Iran de-escalation plus a benign CPI on August 12; a move back to the 100bp area (the rough long-run median neighbourhood) would require either a genuine growth scare or a supply indigestion event, neither of which is currently visible. For HY: 233bp is the record tight and is realistically only reachable on an actual signed Iran deal; the first real warning would be a break back above 300bp, which from 271bp is a 29bp move — historically that has taken either a default-cycle catalyst or an equity drawdown of 5%+. The asymmetry is brutal and simple: at rank 2 and rank 6, you are being paid essentially nothing to own the risk and there is 5.4 percentage points (IG) and 17.0 percentage points (HY) of theoretical downside to the historical maximums. This is not a forecast of widening — momentum is tighter — it is a statement that the reward for owning spread here is as thin as it has ever been.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 61/100 — Sticky, not spiralling — and today's oil crash just bought the Fed real breathing room. The core-trend measures cluster in a tight band well above target but with no acceleration: Median CPI 2.71%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.63%, Median PCE 2.66%, trimmed-mean PCE 2.23%, sticky CPI 2.84% and core sticky CPI 2.81%. Of the seven computed measures, four are flagged falling, two flat, and only one — flexible CPI at 5.06% — is rising, and flexible components are precisely what a 5.7% drop in crude works on first. Core PCE at 3.29% year-over-year remains the uncomfortable headline number and sits meaningfully above the median and trimmed measures, which usually means a handful of large components are doing the damage rather than a broad-based impulse. The Dallas Fed distribution supports that: 23.5% of the PCE basket is rising faster than 5% (8.1% above 10% plus 15.4% in the 5-10% bucket), but 52.4% is running below 2% (24.3% in 0-2% plus 28.1% actually falling) — a barbell, not a wave. Expectations remain anchored and are, if anything, too complacent: the 5-year breakeven at 2.20% and the 1-year at 1.62% say markets expect the near-term inflation pulse to fade fast. Labour costs corroborate the disinflation: average hourly earnings 3.5%, ECI 0.9%, unemployment 4.2%, JOLTS openings 7,359k and quits at 2.2% — a labour market that is loosening without breaking, and a quits rate that low does not generate a wage-price spiral. The one wrinkle is nonfarm payrolls at just 57k against initial claims of only 197k: hiring has slowed sharply but firing has not started. Verdict: the persistence is real and lives in services and shelter, but the direction of travel is gently lower and today's energy move accelerates it. The August 12 CPI is the test.

NEAR TERM

My inference for the next meeting: no change. The market agrees — the 2-year fell 4.6bp to 4.1917% and the 1-year breakeven sits at 1.62%. This is an inference from his record and the data, not a stated Fed intention.

~1 YEAR

Modestly lower policy rates alongside an aggressively smaller balance sheet — and an institution that looks structurally different. Warsh has long argued the Fed should shrink its $6.7 trillion balance sheet, and the reported next step is a concrete target and timeline for shedding assets (CNBC, June 21; U.S. News, June 22). He has also already scrapped forward guidance, stating it 'was not well-suited to the current policy conjuncture' — a genuine break from the Powell era — and has set up five task forces to rethink virtually the entire policy-setting apparatus, described by one account as 'regime change but in a velvet glove'. My twelve-month inference: if the disinflation in the median and trimmed measures continues and payrolls stay near 57k, one or two cuts by mid-2027, but delivered without pre-commitment and paired with faster quantitative tightening — an unusual mix of easier rates and tighter balance sheet. This is my inference from his prior writings and speeches, not a Fed forecast. Flagged clearly: everything in this one-year view is inferred.

The important thing about Warsh for a trader is not the rate path but the removal of the guidance put. A Fed that refuses to pre-commit is a Fed that raises the volatility of every data print — which is one plausible reading of why VIX rose 4.04% to 16.50 on a 1.8% up day into next Wednesday's CPI. His historical writing has been consistently hawkish on the balance sheet and critical of the Fed's expanded footprint, and the Cato reform agenda published for him captures the direction of travel: a narrower institution with a more mechanical framework. The market consequence of faster QT is term premium — and the 30-year at 5.1743% against a -5.66% of GDP deficit is where that shows up first, not in the front end. My reading: the curve at a computed 42.09bps of 2s10s slope is too flat for a world with an accelerating balance-sheet runoff and an unresolved fiscal position. Steepeners are the cleanest Warsh trade. Note that strategists were expecting the Fed on hold for the rest of 2026, which is consistent with the near-term view above.
