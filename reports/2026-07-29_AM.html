🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 64/100 — Sticky, and this morning's oil move makes it stickier. The headline tension in the panel is that Core CPI is running at 2.60% YoY — a number the Fed could live with — while Core PCE, the measure that actually governs policy, is at 3.41%. That is a wide and uncomfortable gap. The underlying-inflation family clusters between 2.41% (Trimmed-Mean PCE) and 2.84% (Sticky CPI), with Median CPI 2.71%, 16% Trimmed-Mean CPI 2.63%, Median PCE 2.82% and Core Sticky CPI 2.81% — a tight band that says the persistent, slow-moving core of inflation has stalled around 2.6-2.8% rather than converging on 2%. The computed core-trend measures all sit in the upper half of their historical distributions (percentiles in the 56-71 range on the sub-score components) with directions split between flat and rising, and only the sticky measures still falling. The breadth data from the Dallas Fed is the part that should worry people: 14.0% of the PCE basket is inflating at more than 10%, another 10.0% at 5-10%, and 34.0% at 3-5% — that is 58% of consumption inflating above 3%, against only 9.6% in the 0-2% bucket and 27.2% outright falling. This is not a narrow, idiosyncratic inflation problem; it is broad. Flexible CPI at 5.06% and rising is the direct channel through which today's 6.76% WTI move arrives, and with crude 11.61% above its 200-day average and not yet technically stretched, that impulse is more likely to build than fade over the next two prints. The counterweight is the market's own forecast: 5-year breakevens at 2.20% and 1-year at 1.78% show investors expect this to be transitory, and wage pressure is contained — Average Hourly Earnings 3.50% and ECI at just 0.90% YoY, which is genuinely disinflationary and argues strongly against a wage-price spiral. Net: a services-and-energy inflation problem with broad basket participation but no labor-cost engine behind it, arriving at a Fed whose new chair has staked his credibility on eliminating it. Expect the 2% target to remain out of reach through 2026, and expect Warsh to treat that as unacceptable rather than tolerable.
