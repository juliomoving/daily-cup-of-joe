A benign-looking tape hid a rough session. The S&P 500 closed at 7,709.96, off just 0.18% (-13.59), and the Nasdaq Composite was virtually flat at 26,348.35 (-0.06%), but the Dow fell 464.02 points (-0.85%) to 53,885.10 and the Russell 2000 dropped 0.58% to 3,001.55 — the classic signature of a market carried by a handful of large caps while the median stock is sold. Nine of eleven US sectors were lower on the day; only Energy (+1.59%) and Health Care (+0.14%) and Info Tech (+0.09%) held green, with Industrials, Real Estate (both -0.83%) and Comm Services (-0.73%) at the back. The biggest story was the collision of two forces: crude ripped higher — Brent +4.86% to 83.31 and WTI +2.75% to 77.29 on the reported change — as Iran-Oman talks over Strait of Hormuz shipping routes kept the tanker-risk premium alive, while the US curve sold off hard across the board (2Y +6.4bp to 4.2454%, 10Y +6.5bp to 4.6778%, 30Y +5.6bp to 5.2242%). Higher energy prices plus a hawkish-leaning Fed under Kevin Warsh is exactly the combination that lifts long-end yields and squeezes rate-sensitive equities, which is what Utilities (-0.63% on the day, -4.42% on the month) and Real Estate showed. Europe was the outlier higher, with the Euro Stoxx 50 +0.39% and STOXX 600 +0.16%; Japan was the worst major, the Nikkei -0.93%, even as 10-year JGB yields fell 4.1bp to 2.788%. Versus the prior session, the notable change is the rotation, not the level: yields up, oil up, cyclicals down, and yet the VIX fell 4.17% to 15.15 and credit spreads were essentially unchanged (US IG 0.76%, US HY 2.67%, +1bp). Sentiment reads as complacent-but-fraying — nobody is paying up for protection, and nobody is chasing either.

So the week has been aggressively growth-over-value, and today was a one-day counter-punch. On the month, Financials +5.06%, Energy +5.30% and Info Tech +4.50% lead while Utilities -4.42% and Comm Services -0.46% lag — bond-proxy sectors are being sold as the long end backs up, which is coherent with a 30Y at 5.2242%. Year-to-date the leadership is unambiguous: Energy +27.23%, Info Tech +22.04%, Industrials +16.87%, Materials +11.90%, Real Estate +11.69%, against Utilities +0.35%, Cons Discretionary +2.33% and Comm Services +2.97%. Net read: growth and energy are the barbell, defensives are being liquidated for rate reasons rather than bought for safety, and value ex-energy has no bid.

The honest framing: at the 2nd and 4th percentile there is no compensation for being wrong, so the asymmetry — not the direction — is the trade. Carry is fine until it isn't, and the cost of the option that protects you is near its own historical low.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 62/100 — Inflation is not re-accelerating broadly, but it has stopped falling — and the components that are moving are moving the wrong way. Core PCE at 3.29% year-over-year remains the single most uncomfortable number on the page: it is more than a full point above target and well above Core CPI at 2.60%, which is the gap that has repeatedly fooled people into declaring victory early. The computed core-trend panel shows the median and trimmed-mean family clustered tightly at 2.23%–2.84% with directions split between 'flat' and 'falling' — genuine, if unspectacular, progress in the sticky middle of the basket. Against that, Flexible CPI at 5.06% is explicitly 'rising' with a z-score of 0.56 and the highest percentile in the set (77), and today's 4.86% jump in Brent is precisely the kind of shock that feeds it. The breadth data is the tiebreaker and it argues for patience over confidence: on the Dallas Fed distribution, 29.3% of the PCE basket by weight is rising faster than 3% (8.1% above 10%, 15.4% at 5–10%, 5.8% at 3–5%), while 28.1% is outright falling and 24.3% is running 0–2%. That is a barbell, not a convergence — a large disinflating tail is doing the arithmetic work of hiding a stubborn fast-inflating tail. Markets are on the sanguine side of this: the 5-year breakeven at 2.2269% and, more strikingly, the 1-year at 1.6601% imply investors expect near-term inflation *below* target, which looks like a bet that the energy move reverses. Labour supports the disinflation case at the margin — payrolls of 57k, JOLTS openings at 7,359k, quits at 2.2%, ECI at 0.9% and average hourly earnings at 3.5% describe a cooling but not cracking market with unemployment at 4.2% and claims at just 199k. Bottom line: the wage channel is closing, the goods-and-energy channel just re-opened, and Core PCE at 3.29% gives a hawkish Fed chair all the cover he needs to do nothing.

The market implication is the asymmetry in crude. A signed deal is worth several dollars lower on Brent; a breakdown that closes the Strait is worth considerably more than several dollars higher. That skew, not the level, is what to trade.

NEAR TERM

Hold. On the record, Warsh's first meeting as chair in June 2026 left rates unchanged, with the Fed explicitly citing still-elevated inflation and price stability as the committee's focus. Nothing in today's data argues for a change: Core PCE at 3.29%, Flexible CPI at 5.06% and rising, Brent +4.86% on the session, unemployment at 4.2% and initial claims at just 199k. The base case for the next meeting is another hold with hawkish-leaning language — and, importantly, the reported dot distribution has the risk skewed toward a hike rather than a cut. This is an inference from his June decision and the published projections, not a stated plan.

~1 YEAR

One hold-to-modest-tightening year, with the balance sheet doing the work the funds rate does not. Reporting on the June meeting indicates nine of the eighteen committee members signalled support for higher rates this year, six of them for two quarter-point increases, and that the Fed's own projection anticipates rates a quarter-point higher by end-2026 — a sharp reversal from March, when the committee forecast one cut. All but one participant sees rates flat or higher by year-end. On that basis my twelve-month read is: no cuts, a meaningful (roughly one-in-three) chance of one 25bp hike, and continued balance-sheet normalisation, which his nomination was widely read as signalling. Flagged as inference: Warsh has not committed to a path, and I am extrapolating from his long-standing public scepticism of large central-bank balance sheets and of pre-emptive easing into above-target inflation.

Warsh arrived with a reputation as a hawk and a balance-sheet reformer, was confirmed 54-45 on 13 May 2026 in the most divisive vote in Fed history, and immediately inherited what CNBC described as a 'family fight' over whether to cut. He did not resolve it by cutting — he resolved it by holding, and by putting price stability at the centre of the first press conference. The market is respecting that: the 2-year at 4.2454% (+6.4bp today) is not the yield of a curve pricing imminent easing, and the whole curve bear-shifted today with 2s10s at a computed 43.24bp. The subtlety worth owning is the balance sheet. A chair who tightens through runoff rather than through the funds rate produces exactly the pattern visible on the screen — a stable front end, a heavy long end (30Y at 5.2242%), and a term premium that grinds wider against a -5.66%-of-GDP budget deficit. That combination is bearish for duration, bearish for bond proxies (US Utilities -4.42% on the month), and, for now, tolerable for equities because credit has not blinked. It stops being tolerable the moment IG moves off 0.76%.
