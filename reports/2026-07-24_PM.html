A quiet index close hid a violent rotation. The S&P 500 finished at 7,411.98, up just 3.68 points (+0.05%), while the Dow added 235.60 (+0.46%) to 51,947.25 — and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 161.87 (-0.64%) to 24,975.82. That gap is the whole story: money left large-cap technology and went almost everywhere else. The Russell 2000 slipped 0.35% to 2,930.00, so this was not a broad risk-off; it was a leadership problem. Overseas the split was sharper still: the Euro Stoxx 50 rose 1.14%, the STOXX 600 0.82% and the FTSE 100 0.91%, while the Nikkei 225 collapsed 1,811.45 points (-2.73%) to 64,611.15 — Japan's 10-year JGB yield rose 2.6bp to 2.816% even as every other major yield fell, a rare and unwelcome combination of higher local rates and lower local equities. Treasuries firmed modestly (2Y -1.6bp to 4.331%, 10Y -1.6bp to 4.677%, 30Y -0.2bp to 5.157%), gilts led the global rally (-7.0bp to 5.032%) and Bunds fell 3.1bp to 3.172%. Crude was the day's other headline: WTI -3.12% and Brent -2.35% to $98.32, backing off despite an Iran conflict now in its fifth month — a market pricing some supply relief, not peace. Gold ($4,052.79, +0.08%) and silver (+1.00%) were steady, copper +0.24%. The VIX eased to 18.58 from 18.70, which tells you the equity market read today's tech air-pocket as rotation rather than rupture. Sentiment: constructive on the surface, brittle underneath — because the two things that could break it (a hawkish Warsh Fed decision on Wednesday and a war that keeps oil near $100) both land inside the next seven days, and credit is priced for neither.

📊Markets

Breadth, not level, is what changed today. The S&P 500's +0.05% close (7,411.98) sat on top of a -0.88% day for US Info Tech and a +2.36% day for Real Estate — an eleven-sector spread of over three points on a session the headline called unchanged. The Dow's +0.46% and the Nasdaq's -0.64% are the same trade seen from two ends. Japan was the outlier that deserves attention: -2.73% on the Nikkei with the 10-year JGB the only major sovereign yield to rise (+2.6bp to 2.816%), while USD/JPY was pinned at 163.83. That is a domestic re-pricing, not a dollar story. Europe took the baton with the Euro Stoxx 50 +1.14%, STOXX 600 +0.82% and FTSE 100 +0.91%, helped by a 7.0bp gilt rally to 5.032% and a 3.1bp Bund rally to 3.172%. In the US, the curve stayed positively sloped at 34.65bp of 10s-over-2s (computed) with the long end sticky — the 30Y barely moved at 5.157%, so the day's duration rally was concentrated in the front and belly, consistent with a market that is nervous about growth but not yet convinced the Fed will help. Commodities cut the other way: WTI -3.12% to $89.31 and Brent -2.35% to $98.32 gave back part of the war premium, and with Energy still +30.3% year-to-date and +11.97% over the past month, that unwind is precisely what took the wind out of the cyclical trade's best-performing leg. Volatility fell (VIX 18.58, -0.12) and credit was almost unchanged. Net: a rotation day inside a market whose index level flatters its internals.

Those are not sector wobbles; those are the long-duration, consumer-facing, mega-cap-heavy parts of the index unwinding together. 🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 69/100 — Sticky, broad, and no longer improving — this is the report's central macro fact. Core PCE at 3.41% YoY is well above target and is the highest-frequency official number on the panel; core CPI at 2.6% and median CPI at 2.71% look better, but the gap between them tells you goods-and-services composition is doing the flattering, not underlying pressure. The computed core-trend measures sit clustered in the mid-to-high percentiles of their own histories with z-scores between -0.12 and +0.22 and directions that are mostly 'flat' — flat at an elevated level is not disinflation, it is a plateau. Two of the seven are actively rising (trimmed-mean PCE at 2.41% and, more worryingly, flexible CPI at 5.06% with the highest z-score on the panel at +0.56), and only the sticky measures are falling. Breadth is the part that should end the debate: per the Dallas Fed distribution, 58% of the PCE basket is inflating at more than 3% annualised (34% in the 3-5% bucket, 10% in 5-10%, 14% above 10%), while only 9.6% is running in the 0-2% zone and 27.2% is outright falling. An inflation problem confined to a few categories is a relative-price story; one where more than half the basket runs above 3% is a monetary story, and this is the latter. Wages give partial comfort — average hourly earnings at 3.5% and ECI at 0.9% are consistent with target once productivity is allowed for — but the market's own expectations are split in an awkward way: the 5-year breakeven at 2.257% versus the 1-year at 1.925% says investors expect a near-term dip and a medium-term re-acceleration. That is the profile of a market that does not believe the last mile gets done. Add Brent at $98.32 and flexible CPI already at 5.06%, and the energy channel is a live upside risk rather than the disinflationary tailwind it was. Verdict: persistence is high, the direction is sideways-to-worse, and the burden of proof has shifted to the doves. Warsh's Fed will read the same panel and it will not encourage them to cut.

The most important thing about the Warsh Fed for a portfolio is not the level of rates — it is the removal of the put. Warsh has emphasised political independence and foreclosed the cuts President Trump has sought, framing the job around delivering 2% rather than around supporting activity or asset prices. That is a genuine regime change from the reaction function markets have traded for fifteen years, and almost nothing in this report's pricing reflects it. IG spreads at the 3rd percentile, HY at the 7th, the VIX at 18.58 and financial conditions at NFCI -0.55 all embed an assumption that the central bank steps in on weakness. Under a chair who has publicly staked his credibility on not doing that, the correct discount is wider spreads and higher volatility at every level of growth. Note the tension in his own record: reporting from his nomination in February framed the appointment as signalling an aggressive balance-sheet pivot and 2026 rate normalisation, with at least one macro commentator predicting four or five cuts — and Yahoo Finance has documented Wall Street waiting for exactly those cuts. Those expectations came from his pre-nomination commentary; his conduct in office since May has run the other way. Where a market has been positioned for one Warsh and is receiving another, the repricing tends to happen in the asset class with the least cushion — which is credit, not equities. His first global speech on July 1 was received positively on the reform agenda, which buys him room to be hawkish. Expect him to use it.
