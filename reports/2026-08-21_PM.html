Expect core PCE to grind lower toward the high-2s over the next two quarters, with the risk skewed higher if Brent holds above $90. This is not a Fed that cuts; it is a Fed that waits, and possibly one that hikes if flexible-component inflation bleeds into the sticky measures.

The asymmetry to understand is that Warsh has more room to hike than a Fed chair with a dovish reputation would, precisely because his credibility is not in question — but he also has more incentive to cut early if unemployment breaks meaningfully above 4.1%, because he would not be accused of capitulating to political pressure. Separately, his on-record advocacy of balance-sheet reduction means the quantity channel may do work the rate channel does not; watch for QT signalling as an alternative to hikes. That last point is an inference from his prior writings, flagged as such.

Warsh's public record is unusually consistent for a Fed chair, which makes him more predictable than the market's positioning implies. His stated priorities are tighter inflation discipline, streamlined Fed communication and a more narrowly focused central bank — and prior to his appointment he urged rate cuts paired with aggressive balance-sheet reduction as a package, a 'regime change' framing that is frequently misread as dovish. It is not. The intellectual core is that the Fed's balance sheet, not the funds rate, is the source of excess accommodation; on that framework, you can be simultaneously open to a lower policy rate and structurally hawkish on liquidity. At his first press conference after leaving rates unchanged, he underscored the Fed's determination to bring inflation down to the 2% target. Where we are inferring rather than reporting: the specific probabilities above, the reading that QT may substitute for hikes, and the judgement that he is more likely to tolerate an equity drawdown than his predecessor. The immediate test is Thursday 27 August, when he is scheduled to speak on monetary policy at the Jackson Hole symposium.
