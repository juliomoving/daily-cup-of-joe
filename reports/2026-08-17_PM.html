The tape on Monday 17 August (close, 17:32 ET) was a textbook energy-shock session: Brent surged 2.89% to $91.08 and WTI 2.55% to $84.50 as the Strait of Hormuz standoff hardened, and every other risk asset paid for it. The S&P 500 closed 7,745.06, down 0.52% (-40.70 pts) from 7,785.76; the Dow shed 0.51% (-272.63) to 53,459.78; the Nasdaq Composite was the relative winner at -0.32% (26,644.91) and the Russell 2000 fell 0.35% to 3,057.54. Volatility woke up more than price did — the VIX jumped 6.60% to 15.19, the sharpest percentage move on the board outside crude. Europe was quieter (Euro Stoxx 50 -0.14%, STOXX 600 -0.22%, FTSE 100 -0.28%) and Japan was the lone outright winner, the Nikkei +0.74% to 69,220.25. The second story is the long end: the US 30-year rose 4.67bp to 5.306% and the 10-year 2.97bp to 4.7219%, while the 2-year barely budged (+0.62bp to 4.1753%) — a pure bear steepening that says the market is pricing an oil-driven inflation term premium, not near-term Fed tightening. Bunds (3.221%), JGBs (2.921%) and Gilts (5.060%) all followed the US higher. What did NOT move is the tell: US HY spread unchanged at 2.66%, EUR IG unchanged at 0.77%, EM USD unchanged at 1.60%, US IG a single basis point wider at 0.79%, and the dollar down 0.09% to 99.574 with EUR/USD 1.1578 and USD/JPY 159.41. Gold was flat at $4,415.99. Sentiment is therefore best described as complacent-with-a-twitch: equities and vol are registering the oil risk, credit and FX are ignoring it entirely, and the bond market is quietly repricing the inflation tail.

🔄Sector Analysis

This was a defensive-fails, energy-wins session, which is an unusual and slightly worrying combination. In the US, Energy was the only sector up on the day (+0.87%) and it is compounding: +3.45% on the week, +8.39% on the month and +36.13% year-to-date — comfortably the best performer in both the US and global panels. Consumer discretionary bleeding 3.25% in a week going into the retail earnings wave (Walmart, Home Depot, Target, Lowe's, TJX, Ross all report) is a positioning signal — the market is pre-emptively marking down the tariff-and-gasoline squeeze on the household. That is a two-horse market. Read across all four windows: capital is concentrated in the two themes that benefit from the current regime — the energy shock and the AI capex cycle — and is actively fleeing anything that depends on the consumer's marginal dollar. Growth over value, but only a very specific slice of growth.

EUR IG unchanged at 0.77% (6th percentile, z -0.89), EUR HY a basis point tighter at 2.78% (7th percentile, z -0.74), and EM USD unchanged at 1.60% with a z-score of -1.38 and a percentile rank of 3 — the single most extreme valuation in the entire dataset, versus a historical range of 1.39% to 10.87%. Every one of the four crisis comparisons (GFC 2008, Euro debt 2011, COVID 2020, hiking cycle 2022) reads 0 for all five indices, i.e. today's spreads are nowhere near any prior stress episode. So what are the levels? For IG, 71bp is the floor — there are 8bp of upside left in the best case, against a mean-reversion target of roughly 100-110bp on any growth scare and 150bp+ if the energy shock genuinely bites margins. For HY, 233bp is the floor, 33bp away, against 350-400bp on a credible recession signal. EM USD at 160bp has 21bp to its all-time tight and would trade 250bp+ on a genuine Hormuz supply disruption, given the energy-importing EM sovereign complex is the direct casualty of $91 Brent. The conditions to reach those wider levels: (1) a sustained Brent print above $100 that forces a real earnings downgrade cycle, (2) a US payroll trend that turns decisively negative — note nonfarm payrolls already printed -23k — or (3) a Fed that hikes into the oil shock rather than looking through it, which Warsh's committee has explicitly floated. None of those is priced. At a z-score of -0.9 with an unchanged daily print on a day crude rose 2.89%, credit is not analysing the risk; it is asleep. Asymmetry strongly favours buying protection here rather than harvesting the last 20-30bp of carry.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 61/100 — Underlying inflation is grinding lower, but the improvement is narrow, slow, and now running straight into an energy shock. The core-trend family all sits in the mid-2s and all of it is falling: Median CPI 2.7%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.6%, Median PCE 2.66%, trimmed-mean PCE 2.23%, core sticky CPI 2.72%. Every one of those measures is tagged falling in the computed panel, which is genuine progress. But three things argue against declaring victory. First, all of those readings still sit in the 55th-65th percentile of their own histories — mid-2s is not 2%, and the market is treating a decelerating 2.7% as if it were a solved problem. Second, headline core PCE at 3.29% YoY sits materially above the entire core-trend family, and flexible CPI at 4.68% (75th percentile, the highest z-score in the panel at 0.49) is where the energy shock lands first. That gap between the sticky measures (2.72%) and the flexible measures (4.68%) is the whole story: the disinflation is in the slow-moving components and the reflation is in the fast-moving ones, and Brent at $91.08 feeds the fast ones. Third, breadth is not clean. The Dallas Fed trimmed distribution shows 8.1% of the PCE basket still rising faster than 10% and another 15.4% rising 5-10% — nearly a quarter of the basket running at multiples of target. Offsetting that, 28.1% of the basket is outright falling and 24.3% is in the 0-2% bucket, so the mass is on the benign side. Net: the disinflation trend is real and intact, but it is mid-2s not low-2s, it is being carried by a shrinking set of components, and an oil shock of this size has historically added 30-60bp to headline within two quarters. The labour side gives the Fed cover to be patient — payrolls at -23k, unemployment 4.1%, quits at 2.2%, average hourly earnings 3.2% and ECI at 0.9% are all consistent with no wage-price spiral.
