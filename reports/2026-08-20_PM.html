The story of the session was a bear-steepening bond market colliding with a bid crude tape, and equities losing the argument. The S&P 500 closed at 7,641.16, down 66.82 points or 0.87%; the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.00% to 26,067.17; the Dow dropped 703.84 points (1.32%) to 52,759.21; and the Russell 2000 was the worst of the majors at -1.34% to 2,992.43, slipping back below the 3,000 handle. VIX rose 1.12 points (7.52%) to 16.01 — a move in fear, not a panic in it. Under the surface the tell was rates: the US 10-year added 5.75bp to 4.7041% and the 30-year 5.65bp to 5.2476%, while the 2-year rose only 2.52bp to 4.1875%. That is the long end selling off on supply, term premium and oil — not a growth scare — and it is the kind of steepening that taxes long-duration equity multiples first. WTI climbed 2.89% to $86.83 and Brent 1.67% to $93.15 as the Strait of Hormuz remained effectively closed to Western-allied shipping; Energy was the only S&P sector with a meaningful gain (+0.38%, +4.15% on the week, +38.67% YTD). Overseas the split was stark: the Nikkei 225 rose 1.36% to 66,216.79 with the 10-year JGB down 4.2bp to 2.860%, the FTSE 100 was flat (+0.04%), Euro Stoxx 50 fell 0.35% and the STOXX 600 was essentially unchanged at -0.12%. Versus the prior session, this was a rotation out of everything long-duration and defensive-priced and into the one thing the war is actually repricing — barrels. Sentiment reads as orderly de-risking rather than distribution: FX did nothing at all (EUR/USD 1.1679, USD/JPY unchanged at 159.05, DXY +0.04% at 98.874), and credit did less than nothing — US IG OAS tightened a basis point to 0.80% and US HY tightened five to 2.70%. When stocks fall 1% and high yield tightens, the equity move is a positioning event, not a solvency event. The macro backdrop is what should keep you honest: nonfarm payrolls printed -23k, unemployment is 4.1%, and core PCE is still running 3.29% year-on-year with a federal deficit at 5.99% of GDP. That is stagflationary arithmetic, and it is exactly the combination a new Fed chair who has abolished forward guidance is least likely to bail you out of.
