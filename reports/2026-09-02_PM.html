Five charts tracking the mechanism Simon White laid out for why Treasury liquidity is deteriorating: rising inflation volatility feeds into a higher term premium, which steepens the curve, which raises bond vol, which shows up as worse UST market liquidity. Latest reading is +0.66 vol points (25th percentile of the 12 runs recorded so far). Positive means the 5%-OTM put is pricier than the at-the-money option -- the market paying up for downside-price / higher-yield insurance on long-end Treasuries.

CPI YoY volatility (2yr) is 0.48pp; the article's thesis is that rising inflation vol drags liquidity worse, which this chart is built to let you check month to month.

Fed staff's own DKW decomposition (D'Amico-Kim-Wei, updated Kim-Walsh-Wei) splits the rise in long yields into a policy-uncertainty component (real term premium) and an inflation-uncertainty component -- both rising is the article's read that this move isn't just a rate-expectations story.

IF THIS RHYMES WITH 2000
Nasdaq fell -78.0% peak→trough (2000-03-10 → 2002-10-09, ~31 months); it took ~15 years to reclaim the high.

The useful way to hold this is that 2000 was a valuation bubble and 2026 is a complacency bubble. Investors in 1999 paid 175x for the Nasdaq; today they pay 39.95x. That is a genuine, structural difference and it is why the drawdown template — the 78% Nasdaq collapse from 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 to 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002, over 31 months — is a poor forecast for today. What has replaced expensive equities is cheap insurance and cheap credit. A 15.20 VIX (200% of the 2000 signpost), an NFCI at -0.56 (163%), IG spreads at 0.79% (134%) and Baa–10Y at 154bp (157%) mean the market is charging almost nothing to bear risk that has not gone away. The path-anchored charts frame the setup: the current cycle, anchored at the October-2022 low, sits at 238.6 at month 47, versus the dot-com analogue which by month 47 from its Oct-1990 anchor was at 235.7 and did not peak until month 113 at 1,423.9. Anchored instead at the 1994 pre-internet reset, the analogue was at 252.0 by month 47 and peaked at month 69. In other words, on the 1990 template we are not remotely late; on the 1994 template we would be roughly two years from the top. Neither is a prediction — the analogue is a shape, not a schedule. Where the analogy does bite is concentration and real-asset positioning: a top-10 weight near 40% (analyst estimate) and a commodities-versus-equities ratio scoring 200% of 2000 conditions describe a market where a single leadership group can take the index down and where nobody owns the hedge. The semis multiple at 79% of 2000's is the tell for where that concentration sits. And note the one honest offset in the data — retail sentiment is not euphoric at all: the AAII spread is negative 11.5 points and consumer confidence is 89.4 against 140.8 at the peak. Bubbles do not usually top with the retail investor net bearish. That argues for more time, not less. But it does not argue for cheap protection: the correct trade in a complacency bubble is to own the volatility and the credit hedges the market refuses to price, and to keep owning the real assets — energy at 41.08% year to date is telling you the regime already turned, even if index-level valuation has not.

NEAR TERM

~1 YEAR

Over the next twelve months I expect a policy rate modestly higher than today's, not lower — roughly 25–50bp of net tightening (analyst estimate), with the larger and more consequential change coming through the balance sheet rather than the funds rate. This is an inference from Warsh's long-standing on-record positions rather than from anything he has announced: his consistent argument as a Fed governor and as a critic since has been that the balance sheet is the distortion, that quantitative easing blurred the line between monetary and fiscal policy, and that shrinking it is the route back to a credible, smaller Fed. Expect the emphasis on framework reform and on visibly re-establishing independence from Treasury — CNBC's Jackson Hole roundup explicitly noted his stance may put the Fed 'at odds' with Treasury, which is a datapoint about institutional conflict, not just rates. Risks to this view run both ways: a genuine labour-market break (payrolls turning decisively negative for several months) forces cuts regardless of philosophy, while Brent sustained above $100 pushing the flexible-price CPI — already at 4.68% — higher would force more than 50bp. I am flagging clearly that the one-year path is my inference from his prior writings and speeches; he has not laid out a twelve-month plan.

Warsh was confirmed 54-45 on 13 May 2026, the most divisive Fed confirmation vote on record, taking office when Powell's term expired on 15 May. His Jackson Hole debut in late August was read as unexpectedly hawkish, with the line 'it's hard to say that Fed policy is restrictive when you look at the economy right now' interpreted by analysts as a straight hike signal. That single sentence moved September hike odds from roughly 40% a week earlier to the mid-to-high 50s. The intellectual position is coherent even if you disagree with it: core PCE at 3.34% is more than a full point above target, financial conditions are computed as loose (NFCI -0.56), credit spreads are at the 3rd–5th percentile of history, margin debt is growing 45.6% year over year and equities sit at a 2.87 z-score stretch. The genuine tension for markets is that Warsh appears to weight financial conditions and Fed credibility more heavily than the standard dual-mandate output gap — which means he is likelier than a conventional chair to tighten into weak employment data if he judges that markets are pricing risk too cheaply. Note one contrarian view in the coverage: at least one investor argues the market has the September call wrong. Given today's price action — a 6.98% VIX drop and a 3.1bp front-end rally into a meeting where a hike is more likely than not — the market is not hedging this properly.
