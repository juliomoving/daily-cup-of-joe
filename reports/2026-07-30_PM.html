🧮Fixed Income Spreadscomputed

Corporate credit is priced for a world without a war. US IG option-adjusted spread — the extra yield lenders demand over Treasuries — is 80bp, a computed z-score of −0.80 and the 5th percentile of its history, versus an all-time tight of 70bp and a historic wide of 620bp. US HY is 289bp (z −0.81, 11th percentile) against a 230bp record tight and 1,970bp crisis wide. Europe mirrors it: EUR IG 79bp (8th percentile), EUR HY 293bp (12th percentile). EM USD at 168bp is the richest of the lot — z −1.31, 5th percentile, against a 140bp record tight — which is remarkable given the Strait of Hormuz is closed and EM is where the energy import bill lands. Versus every crisis benchmark the computed panel shows 0% of the way to GFC 2008, Euro-debt 2011 or COVID 2020 levels across all five indices; US HY alone registers a 2% move toward its 2022 hiking-cycle wides. Key levels and what it takes to get there. IG: 100bp is the first line that would signal genuine repricing — it needs either a sustained oil shock feeding a Q3 margin squeeze or a Fed hike that breaks the refinancing math; below 70bp we are at an all-time record and the asymmetry is absurd. HY: 350bp is the level where high-yield stops being a rates trade and starts being a credit trade — that requires a default-cycle catalyst (energy-driven input costs, or the 8.1% net share of banks already tightening standards per SLOOS accelerating). The realistic path to 400bp+ is a closed Hormuz for a full quarter with a Fed that cannot cut. What you are being paid today, at 289bp for the whole US HY market, is roughly 2.9 percentage points a year to underwrite that scenario. That is not enough. The trade is not to short credit outright — carry kills you — it is to stop paying up for spread and to own the optionality elsewhere.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 66/100 — Sticky, broadening at the edges, and now with a live energy accelerant — this is not a disinflation you can extrapolate. The core-trend measures that strip out noise are clustered tightly in the 2.6–2.8% range (Median CPI 2.71%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.63%, Median PCE 2.66%, Core Sticky CPI 2.81%), all sitting in the 56th–70th percentile of their own history and all flat-to-falling — genuinely encouraging. But three things argue against declaring victory. First, headline Core PCE at 3.29% YoY sits well above every one of those underlying gauges, which means the gap is being carried by the volatile components, and Flexible CPI at 5.06% (76th percentile, RISING, z +0.56) is exactly where a closed Strait of Hormuz shows up first. Second, breadth is worse than the averages: the Dallas Fed distribution has 8.1% of the PCE basket rising faster than 10% and 15.4% rising 5–10% — nearly a quarter of the basket in outright high inflation — against only 28.1% actually falling and 24.3% in the 0–2% zone. Third, the market has stopped believing in the near term: the 1-year breakeven is 1.741% while the 5-year is 2.256%, an inversion that says investors expect a near-term dip and a medium-term re-acceleration. Wages are not the problem (AHE 3.5%, ECI 0.9%). Energy is. My persistence score of 66 reflects a core that is genuinely converging toward target being overwhelmed by a supply shock the core measures are designed to ignore.

~1 YEAR

Two regimes, and which one you get depends almost entirely on oil. Base case (my estimate, ~55%): one hike in the second half of 2026 taking the funds rate to roughly 3.75–4.00%, held into 2027, with balance-sheet runoff continuing or accelerating — Warsh's long-standing agenda is a materially SMALLER balance sheet, and he can deliver that regardless of what he does with rates. Alternative (~30%): if the energy shock resolves and Flexible CPI rolls back over toward the 2.2–2.8% cluster the underlying measures already show, he pivots to the lower-rates-over-time path most associated with him, delivering perhaps 50–75bp of cuts by mid-2027 while framing it as vindication rather than accommodation. Tail (~15%): a genuine inflation re-acceleration above 5% forces 100bp+ of hikes and a credit accident — the scenario the 80bp IG spread is not paying you for. All three probabilities are analyst estimates. One structural point worth watching regardless: Warsh has signalled a rethink of how the Fed measures inflation, which given the current 3.29% Core PCE versus ~2.7% trimmed-mean gap is not an academic exercise — a framework that leans on underlying measures would let him cut sooner than the headline allows.

Warsh inherited the worst possible hand for a man with his reputation. His agenda — lower rates over time, a smaller balance sheet, and a rethink of inflation measurement — was written for a disinflationary world, and he arrived in one where inflation hit a three-year high of 4.2% in May, pushed up by the Iran war's effect on gasoline prices. His response has been to lead with independence and inflation-fighting, which is both the credible move and the one that disappoints the President who appointed him. That tension is the single most important thing in US macro right now, and it is why the 30Y sold off today (5.2135%, +1.3bp) while the 2Y rallied (4.2459%, −2.7bp): the front end trusts him, the long end is pricing the fiscal and political consequences of him being right. For portfolios, the practical implication is that the 'Fed put' is struck much lower than equity markets behaved today. A VIX at 17.09 and IG spreads at 80bp are consistent with an easing central bank. This one may be about to hike. I am flagging clearly that the near-term and one-year calls above are my inferences from his on-record positions and current data — he has not pre-committed to a path.
