A textbook late-cycle session: the index barely moved, but what moved inside it mattered. The S&P 500 closed at 7,728.20, down 24.91 points (-0.32%) from Monday's 7,753.11; the Nasdaq Composite was the weak leg at 26,445.45, off 159.91 (-0.60%); the Dow shed 184.13 (-0.34%) to 53,791.85. The Russell 2000 went the other way, +9.72 (+0.32%) to 3,027.12 — a small-cap bid against mega-cap distribution. The VIX slipped to 15.28 (-0.18), which is the tell: this was rotation, not fear. Europe closed mixed-to-firm (Euro Stoxx 50 +0.24% at 6,551.22, STOXX 600 +0.01% at 660.51, FTSE 100 -0.17% at 10,844.19). The Nikkei print of 66,970.22 (+2.08%) is a stale Monday Aug-10 close and should not be read as today's Japan tape. The biggest story is the split between the war premium and the equity complacency financing it: Brent added 1.80% to $89.30 and WTI 1.30% to $83.20 as Iran attached conditions to reopening the Strait of Hormuz, S&P Energy led sectors at +1.06% on the day and +32.98% year-to-date, while Communication Services fell -2.12% on the day and -4.84% on the week. Treasuries rallied modestly across the curve — 2Y -2.7bp to 4.2138%, 10Y -1.8bp to 4.6883%, 30Y -1.1bp to 5.2403% — with Bunds -2.1bp to 3.158% and gilts -2.5bp to 4.964%. Credit did nothing at all: US IG OAS unchanged at 77bp, US HY +1bp to 265bp, EM USD +2bp to 161bp. Sentiment reads as complacent-constructive rather than euphoric: nobody is paying up for protection, the dollar is inert (DXY 99.823, +0.01%), and gold is pinned at 4,369.89 (-0.005%). The uncomfortable fact underneath it is a Nonfarm Payroll print of -23k against 4.1% unemployment, a Core PCE still at 3.29% year-over-year, and a Flexible CPI running 5.06% and rising — an economy losing jobs while its energy-sensitive prices reheat, priced by a credit market at its 2nd-to-3rd historical percentile of risk compensation.

The computed trend statistics describe a market that is extended, not broken — which is the hardest configuration to trade. The S&P 500 at 7,728.20 sits 3.03% above its 50-day moving average and 9.46% above its 200-day, with a computed percentile rank of 100 and a z-score of 2.96 — the stretch versus its own history is as extreme as this series has recorded. The Nasdaq is the same story: 2.01% above the 50-day, 9.61% above the 200-day, percentile rank 100, z-score 3.01. Critically, no moving-average cross is signalling on any asset — the trend structure is intact and the trigger has not fired. What the reversal-frequency stats tell you is how much a cross would be worth if it did. For the S&P, 33% of the last 9 upside (golden) crosses and 44% of the last 9 downside (death) crosses were followed by a change in trend — so a death cross here would be roughly a 44% probability of an actual trend reversal, i.e. a coin-flip you should respect but not obey blindly. The Nasdaq is far more asymmetric: only 20% of its last 10 golden crosses reversed, but 60% of its last 10 death crosses did — meaning downside crosses in the Nasdaq have historically been the more informative signal by a factor of three, and that is the single most actionable technical fact in this report given a z-score of 3.01. Gold is the outlier: 4,370.11 is 5.34% above its 50-day but still 2.83% *below* its 200-day, percentile rank 98, z-score 3.42 — a short-term momentum thrust inside an unrepaired longer-term downtrend, with golden crosses reversing 54% of the time and death crosses 50%, i.e. essentially no historical edge either way. The US 10Y at 4.6883% is 3.0% above its 50-day and 8.83% above its 200-day (rank 97, z 1.65), with death crosses in yield reversing 60% of the time against 38% for golden crosses — the historical bias favours yields eventually falling from stretched highs. WTI at 83.20 is 3.67%/8.37% above its 50-/200-day with a rank of 69 and a z-score of only 0.49: the least statistically extended move on the board, which is precisely why the energy trade still has room. EUR/USD (rank 41, z -0.50) is the only genuinely neutral chart here.

Every single index reads 0% of the way to GFC-2008, Euro-debt-2011, COVID-2020 and Hike-2022 crisis levels — there is no stress in this market at all, which is exactly the problem. The asymmetry is arithmetic, not opinion: US IG sits 6bp above its all-time tight and 541bp below its wide; US HY sits 32bp above its tight and 1,706bp below its wide. You are being offered roughly 30bp of upside for taking 1,700bp of theoretical downside. Key levels to watch: US IG through 71bp or US HY through 233bp would be new all-time tights and a genuine capitulation signal for the last credit bears — likely requiring a confirmed Fed easing path plus a Hormuz de-escalation. On the other side, US IG through 90bp and US HY through 320-340bp would mark the first real repricing; that requires a catalyst credit does not currently discount, and the two candidates are visible in this dataset: a payroll trend that has already gone negative (-23k) turning into rising default expectations, or an oil-driven inflation re-acceleration (Flexible CPI 5.06% and rising) that forces the Warsh Fed to hike into a slowing labour market. The trigger to watch first is EM USD at 161bp with a z-score of -1.37 — the tightest asset relative to its own history, in the region physically closest to the war.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 58/100 — Two-speed and getting harder to call. The slow-moving core measures are genuinely cooling: Median PCE (2.66%), Trimmed-Mean PCE (2.23%), Sticky CPI (2.84%) and Core Sticky CPI (2.81%) are all tagged as falling in the computed core-trend panel, and Core CPI is down at 2.6%. But every one of those measures still sits in the upper half of its own historical distribution — the computed core-trend sub-score (rendered separately) confirms that this is disinflation from an elevated base, not a return to normal. Against that, the fast-moving side is re-accelerating hard: Flexible CPI is running 5.06% and is the only measure in the panel tagged rising, with the highest percentile rank (77) and z-score (0.56) in the set — that is the war showing up in prices, with Brent at 89.30 and up 1.80% today. Core PCE at 3.29% year-over-year remains the number that matters for policy and it is more than a full point above target. Breadth from the Dallas Fed distribution is the swing factor: 23.5% of the PCE basket is rising faster than 5% (8.1% above 10%, 15.4% in the 5-10% band), against 28.1% actually falling and 24.3% in the 0-2% band — so roughly a quarter of the basket is in outright inflation and roughly half is at or below target. That is not a broad-based inflation, it is a concentrated energy-and-goods shock sitting on top of a decelerating core. Markets side with the doves: the 5-year breakeven is 2.2524% and the 1-year is 1.7519%, both below target, and the 1y-below-5y shape says investors expect the oil shock to pass. My verdict is that they are probably right on the horizon but wrong on the path — the risk is a Fed chair who has said supply shocks should be looked through, presiding over a committee that has signalled hikes, while Flexible CPI runs at 5% into a labour market that just printed -23k jobs.
