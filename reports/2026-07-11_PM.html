“Hormuz Shut, Yet the Tape Yawns: Complacency Meets a Closed Strait”

Friday's close (the last full print; everything below is PREV CLOSE · Fri Jul 10) left US equities grinding higher into a war headline — the S&P 500 +0.42% to 7,575.39, the Nasdaq Composite +0.29% to 26,281.61, the Dow +0.29% to 52,637.01 — while the VIX sagged 5.11% to 15.03. The biggest story is the dissonance: the IRGC navy has declared the Strait of Hormuz closed until further notice after a commercial vessel was struck, the US has now run a third round of strikes with roughly 90 targets hit, and yet WTI fell 0.93% to $71.41 and Brent 0.38% to $76.01. Either the market has priced an Omani-brokered corridor deal (Araghchi is reportedly carrying such a proposal home), or it is asleep. Beneath the surface the tape is not uniformly brave: Russell 2000 fell 0.49% while megacap indices rose, Health Care led losses (-0.81% 1d, -1.85% 1w) and gold sits 5.36% below its 50-day and 8.28% below its 200-day — a hedge that is being sold, not bought. Rates offered the other tell: US 2Y +3.1bp to 4.2079% and 10Y +1.0bp to 4.5612% while the 30Y slipped 0.6bp to 5.0587%, a bear-flattening at the front that says the inflation problem, not the war, is what the front end fears. Credit is priced for perfection — US IG OAS 0.76%, HY 2.63%, EM 1.60%, all in the low single-digit percentiles of their own history (computed). Sentiment: constructive-to-complacent, with the risk premium for a closed chokepoint conspicuously missing.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 72/100 — Sticky, broad, and being ignored. The headline core numbers look almost respectable — Core CPI 2.9% YoY, Median CPI 2.85%, Median PCE 2.82% — but every one of those measures sits in the upper third of its own historical distribution (computed percentile ranks of 62 to 77) and four of the seven are computed as 'rising', none as 'falling'. Core PCE at 3.41% YoY is the number that matters and it is not at target. The breadth data is what turns a sticky read into a persistent one: the Dallas Fed distribution shows 14.0% of the PCE basket inflating at more than 10%, another 10.0% at 5–10%, and 34.0% at 3–5% — that is 58% of the basket running at or above 3%, with only 27.2% actually falling. This is not a few outliers dragging an average; it is a majority of the basket. Sticky CPI (3.12%, 76th percentile, rising) and Core Sticky CPI (3.09%, 77th percentile, rising) confirm the persistence is in the slow-moving components — the ones that do not respond to a $71 oil price. Meanwhile Flexible CPI is at 6.99% (85th percentile, rising), so the fast-moving components are accelerating too, and with the Strait of Hormuz declared closed, the goods/energy channel is a live upside risk rather than the disinflationary tailwind it has been. The offsets are real but thin: wage pressure is contained (Avg Hourly Earnings 3.5%, ECI 0.9% YoY), the labour market is loosening (Unemployment 4.2%, Nonfarm Payroll just 57k, JOLTS openings 7,594k), and the market simply does not believe in an inflation problem — the 1y breakeven at 1.3112% is extraordinary, well below the 5y at 2.2991%, meaning investors expect inflation to be lower over the next year than over five. That gap is the trade. If the labour data is right, the payrolls print of 57k eventually breaks the sticky components. If the breadth data is right, it does not — and a closed chokepoint plus a Fed chair who has already told you higher rates are ahead is a genuinely uncomfortable combination. I lean toward persistence: the disinflation is narrow, the inflation is broad.

Equity Froth is the least alarming: the Nasdaq's trailing P/E of 39.87x is only 16% of the way to the 2000 peak of 175x, and the price stretch versus the 200DMA (10.5%) is 21% of the way to the 50% stretch seen in 2000. Macro Regime is mixed: the dollar at 101.0 is 37% of the way to the 120 of 2000, but commodities-versus-equities computes to 200% of the 2000-peak condition — capital is more crowded into equities relative to real assets than it was in March 2000, full stop. Bear-Market Signposts are the loudest: VIX complacency computes to 200% of the 2000 condition (a 15.03 print against a 23.1 peak-era analogue), financial conditions (NFCI -0.51) to 155%, SLOOS to 89%, put/call to 70%, margin-debt growth (+40.7% YoY) to 56%. The honest synthesis: this is not a 1999 valuation bubble across the market — the Nasdaq multiple is a quarter of what it was. It is something arguably more dangerous: a narrow leadership bubble (semis at 98%) sitting on top of the most complacent credit and volatility conditions of the modern era (IG 136%, Baa 157%, VIX 200%), with the top-10 S&P weight around 37% versus roughly 23% in 2000. In 2000, the market was expensive and knew it was risky. Today the market is less expensive in aggregate and is priced as though risk does not exist. The 2000 analogue took 31 months to fall 78% from a 5,048.62 peak to a 1,114.11 trough. I would not forecast that. I would say the fuse — narrow leadership plus zero credit cushion plus a 15 VIX — is the same fuse.

NEAR TERM

HOLD at the next meeting, with a hawkish framing — and a materially higher probability of a HIKE than the market is pricing. What is my inference is the timing — I do not expect Warsh to move at the very next meeting, because a chair who has spent his first months building institutional machinery (five task forces) and criticising the Fed's 'swivel chair problem' of reacting to each data release is not a chair who hikes on one CPI print. He will want the framework in place before he uses it. Probability of a hike at the next meeting, analyst estimate: 20%. Probability of a cut: 10%. The near-term event that matters more than the meeting is Wednesday July 15, when Warsh gives his first congressional testimony before the House Financial Services Committee — his first extended, unscripted public exposure as chair, and the highest-variance Fed event of the week.

~1 YEAR

Higher policy rates than the market currently discounts, and a smaller balance sheet. On a ~12-month view I expect one to two hikes, with the risk skewed to more rather than fewer — analyst estimate. The reasoning, drawn from his own record: Warsh has been consistent for over a decade that the Fed has been too easy, too large, and too talkative. He has now named task forces on precisely the five things he intends to change — Fed communications, the balance sheet, the Fed's reliance on existing data sources, productivity and jobs, and the inflation framework (CNN, July 9). The balance-sheet task force is the one the market is under-pricing: a chair who believes the Fed's footprint distorts markets has both the mandate and now the machinery to shrink it, and quantitative tightening under a hawkish chair is a term-premium event that lands in the 10Y (4.5612%, computed 93rd percentile and rising) and the 30Y (5.0587%), not the 2Y. Against him: payrolls at 57k and unemployment at 4.2% give the doves a real argument, and if the labour market cracks he will not hike into it. But with Core PCE at 3.41% and 58% of the PCE basket running above 3%, he has all the cover he needs. The market's 1y breakeven of 1.3112% is, in my view, a direct bet against this man's stated worldview.

The most important thing to understand about Warsh is that he intends to change the Fed's REACTION FUNCTION, not just its rate path — and markets are still trading the old one. His stated project is 'regime change' in how the Fed forecasts and communicates: he has criticised Fed communications as a source of policy error that placed the central bank 'more at the center of market decisions and the economy than it should be,' and at the Hoover Institution he said 'Fed leaders would be well-served to skip opportunities to share their latest musings… The swivel chair problem, rhetorically waxing and waning with the latest data release, is common and counter-productive.' He has already shortened his press conferences relative to his predecessor and hinted at holding fewer of them, noting that 'when you have one, you want to make sure you have something important to say' (CNBC, June 12). His first global outing at the ECB forum in Sintra (July 1) focused on the reaction function itself — the criteria the Fed will use — and drew supportive reviews. The trading implication is underappreciated: LESS forward guidance means MORE realised rate volatility. For a decade the Fed has functioned as a short-volatility position for the entire bond market, telegraphing every move. Warsh is deliberately removing that. Even if he never hikes, the term premium on US duration should rise simply because the information content of the Fed's communication is falling. That, more than any single hike, is the trade — and it argues for a steeper curve (2s10s is only 35.33bps, computed) and against the market's serene 1.3112% one-year breakeven. Watch Wednesday's House testimony closely: it is the first time he must answer questions he did not choose.
