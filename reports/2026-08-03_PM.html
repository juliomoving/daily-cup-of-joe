The single story of the session was de-escalation. After President Trump cancelled a threatened multi-day air campaign against Iran late on 1 August and said negotiations would begin Monday (CNN, Al Jazeera), the war premium came out of crude in a hurry: WTI −5.11% to 80.34 and Brent −4.75% to 83.75, the largest single-session move in the entire data set. Everything risk-positive followed. The S&P 500 closed +1.48% at 7,600.50, the Nasdaq Composite +2.13% at 25,913.90, the Dow +1.32% and the Russell 2000 +1.73% — a broad, high-beta rally rather than a narrow mega-cap grind, with small caps outpacing the Dow. Europe joined (Euro Stoxx 50 +1.08%, STOXX 600 +0.45%) while the FTSE 100 (−0.10%) and Nikkei (−0.94%) lagged, the FTSE dragged by its energy weight and Japan by a rising JGB yield (2.835%, +3bp) that stands alone against a global bond rally. That combination, cheap insurance and no crowd euphoria, is why the melt-up had room to run today. What it does not do is make Wednesday's July CPI print any easier.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 63/100 — Sticky, not accelerating — and the breadth data is worse than the headline. The gap that matters is Core PCE at 3.28653% against Core CPI at 2.6%: those are not telling the same story, and the Fed targets the higher one. Every underlying-trend measure the Fed actually watches clusters in the mid-2s to high-2s — Median CPI 2.71455%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.62829%, Median PCE 2.66027%, trimmed-mean PCE 2.23%, sticky CPI 2.8427% and core sticky CPI 2.8069% — which is a full percentage point above target with no measure showing genuine deceleration below 2.2%. The computed core-trend work has median PCE, trimmed-mean PCE, sticky and core-sticky CPI all falling, and median and trimmed-mean CPI flat, so the direction is mildly favourable, but the *levels* have stalled near 2.7% rather than converging on 2%. The one measure that is rising is flexible CPI at 5.0616% (computed z-score 0.56, 77th percentile) — the volatile, energy-and-goods-sensitive component. Today's 5% crude drop attacks precisely that number, which is the genuinely good news in this panel and should show up in the July print. The Dallas Fed breadth distribution is where I get uncomfortable: 8.1% of the PCE basket is running above 10%, 15.4% between 5% and 10%, and 5.8% between 3% and 5% — so 29.3% of consumption is inflating at 3% or faster, and 23.5% is above 5%. Only 24.3% sits in the 0–2% comfort zone with 28.1% outright falling. Inflation is not broad, but it is deeply entrenched in a specific quarter of the basket, and that concentrated tail is what keeps the median measures pinned near 2.7%. Market pricing disagrees with the data and I side with the data on the near end: the 1-year breakeven at 1.6827% is pricing inflation *below target* over the next twelve months — that looks like a straight energy-price extrapolation of today's crude move, and it is the most mispriced number on this page. The 5-year at 2.2498% is more sensible. Labour supports gradual cooling without collapse — unemployment 4.2%, JOLTS openings 7,594k, initial claims a very low 197k, but nonfarm payrolls at just 57k is a materially weak hiring number. Wage pressure is fading: average hourly earnings 3.5% and the employment cost index at 0.9% is genuinely soft. Verdict: the inflation impulse is decelerating from the top down and from wages, but the floor is around 2.6–2.8%, not 2%. That is a Fed on hold, not a Fed cutting — exactly where Warsh has parked it.

The useful reframing is that today's market and March 2000 are frothy in different places. In 2000 the froth was in *price behaviour* — a Nasdaq 50% above its own 200-day average, retail euphoria at +20.5 net bullish, consumer confidence at 140.8, margin debt growing 72.7%. The crowd was the bubble. Today the Nasdaq is 7.9% above its 200-day, retail is net *bearish* at −11.1, and confidence is 90.8. What is stretched today is the *plumbing*: IG credit at 134% of the 2000 tightness, Baa spreads at 152%, financial conditions 62% looser, VIX complacency 100% beyond the analogue, and commodities-versus-equities at 200%. Money is cheaper and more crowded into equities than it was in 2000; the people holding it are considerably less excited about it. That is a genuinely different animal and it changes the failure mode. A sentiment bubble pops from the inside when the last buyer arrives. A credit-complacency bubble breaks from the outside — a shock arrives and finds nobody was paid to underwrite it. The anchored-drawdown chart makes the point about timing rather than level. Anchored at the October 2022 low, the current cycle is 46 months in and sits at 235.8 versus the 100 base. Anchored at the October 1990 bear-market bottom, the dot-com run had reached only about 235 by month 42 and did not peak until month 113 at 1,423.9 — six more years and another sixfold gain. Anchored at the 1994 reset, the run peaked at month 69 at 665.3. On either anchor, the current advance is nowhere near the *duration* or the *magnitude* of the terminal dot-com phase. If you are looking for the 2000 analogue, we are closer to 1996–97 than to March 2000 on price, and closer to March 2000 than to 1997 on credit. The specific thing I would watch is the semiconductor P/E at 51.99x (80% of the analogue) against long-term EPS growth expectations. The AI capex cycle is the earnings assumption underneath this entire market, and unlike 2000's revenue-less dot-coms, today's leaders genuinely earn money — but they earn it selling to each other's capex budgets. Concentration near 39.5% of the index in ten names means the question is not 'is the market a bubble' but 'is the capex assumption right', and those ten names *are* the market. On the froth/catalyst/regime/signpost framework: froth is elevated but not terminal, catalysts are largely absent except credit, the regime is extreme on capital crowding, and the signposts are contradictory — complacent institutions, unconvinced households. That is not a top. It is a market with no shock absorbers.
