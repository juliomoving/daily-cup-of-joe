🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 58/100 — Sticky but genuinely decelerating — with one honest asterisk. Every single underlying measure in the computed core-trend panel is flagged 'falling': Median CPI 2.70%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.60%, Median PCE 2.66%, trimmed-mean PCE 2.23%, sticky CPI 2.76%, core sticky CPI 2.72%. That is unanimity, and unanimity in the trimmed measures is what a real disinflation looks like, not a statistical fluke in one series. The asterisk is that these sit near their historical middle-to-upper range rather than at benign levels, and headline Core PCE at 3.29% YoY is still more than a full point above target while Core CPI reads 2.50% — a gap that big between the two is unusual and argues the PCE weighting is picking up something the CPI is not. Breadth from the Dallas Fed distribution is the least comfortable part: 8.1% of the PCE basket is still rising faster than 10% and 15.4% is in the 5-10% bucket, so 23.5% of consumer spending faces high-single-digit-or-worse inflation, against 28.1% of the basket outright falling and 24.3% rising less than 2%. That is a barbell, not a convergence. Flexible CPI at 4.68% (the most volatile, most energy-sensitive component) is the outlier at the 75th percentile with a z-score of 0.49 — and with the Strait of Hormuz closed and Brent at 88.49, that is the component most likely to reaccelerate. Market pricing disagrees with all of this worry: the 5-year breakeven sits at 2.2447% and the 1-year at just 1.6841%, meaning the bond market expects inflation below target within a year. Wage costs support the disinflation case — average hourly earnings +3.2% and ECI +0.9% are consistent with 2% inflation given productivity. Bottom line: the trend is down, the level is not yet acceptable, and the risk is entirely on the energy side.

Bubble-analog — today's Nasdaq vs the dot-com cycle (two anchors)

WHY TWO START DATES?
Each line is rebased to 100 at a low the tech bull launched from, so elapsed time lines up. Oct-2022 is today's anchor — it was the prior bear-market bottom. Oct-1990 is the dot-com cycle's equivalent (its literal prior bear bottom). But 1990 predates the internet mania by ~4 years, so the second chart re-anchors the dot-com line to the 1994 reset low, just before the surge ignited, for a tighter like-for-like. Today sits very early under the strict 1990 analog, further along under the 1994 surge analog.

IF THIS RHYMES WITH 2000
Nasdaq fell -78.0% peak→trough (2000-03-10 → 2002-10-09, ~31 months); it took ~15 years to reclaim the high.

The chart framing matters more than any single number. Anchored at the October-1990 bear low, the dot-com run peaked at month 113 having compounded to 1,423.9 — a 14x. Anchored at the 1994 pre-internet reset, it peaked at month 69 at 665.3. Today's cycle, anchored at October-2022, is at month 46 and sits at 242.0 — a 2.4x. On the 1990 template we would be at roughly month 46 of a 113-month advance, i.e. not remotely late; on the tighter 1994 template we would be two-thirds of the way through. That is the whole ambiguity in one comparison, and anyone claiming certainty on the timing is selling something. The composition argument is where I land: the froth in this cycle is concentrated in valuation and in the total absence of risk premia — semis at 92% of 2000 mania multiples, IG credit *tighter* than 2000, a VIX at 14.55 — rather than in price velocity or retail mania. Bubbles that end violently need a catalyst, and the catalyst dimension is the weakest part of the panel: ISM Manufacturing at 55.6 and Services at 54.1 with a positively-sloped curve say the cycle has not turned. So the honest sequencing is that the market can stay expensive for a while, and when it breaks, the break will run through credit — where there is 34bp of cushion in high yield and 7bp in investment grade — not through the earnings cycle. I am flagging clearly: the specific probabilities and the cut count above are my inferences from his prior statements and the reported projection path, not from any guidance he has issued.
