🏦Fed Chair Warsh
NEAR TERM

Hold, with a hawkish tilt and a non-trivial risk of a hike being formally put on the table. This is an inference from Warsh's on-record behaviour, not a reported forecast. The Fed left rates unchanged at the 29 July meeting, and at his first press conference as chair Warsh underscored the Fed's determination to bring inflation to the 2% target, said committee members have 'no tolerance for persistently elevated inflation' and share a 'resolute commitment to restoring price stability', and stated the Fed would not hesitate to act to stop inflation. In June his colleagues on the rate-setting committee signalled support for *higher* rates — a sharp reversal from March, when no policymaker penciled in a hike and the committee forecast one cut for 2026. With Core PCE at 3.29% and an oil shock feeding the Flexible CPI at 4.68%, I see essentially no path to a cut at the next meeting, and I put the probability of a cut in the very low single digits (analyst estimate). The more interesting question is whether the -23k payroll print is enough to stop the hawkish drift.

~1 YEAR

One cut at most, and plausibly zero, with the tail risk skewed toward a hike rather than an easing cycle — an inference, not a reported forecast. The market is not positioned for this: five-year breakevens at 2.25% and one-year at 1.67% imply the bond market expects the energy shock to pass and inflation to settle at or below target, which would eventually license easing. My reading of Warsh's record points the other way. His long-standing critique — from his writings and his tenure as a governor during and after the financial crisis — has been that the Fed's balance sheet is too large, that its forward guidance over-promises, and that credibility is the central bank's only real asset. That framework is already visible in how he has changed the institution: the policy statement is much shorter than under previous leadership, forward guidance has been curtailed, and he pointedly declined to submit his own interest-rate projection in the June dot plot. A chair who removes the market's guardrails is a chair who intends to keep optionality to move against consensus. Add his likely instinct to shrink the balance sheet, and the risk to the long end is that quantitative tightening runs alongside a 5.99%-of-GDP deficit. The offsetting scenario is the labour market: if the -23k payroll print becomes a trend and unemployment rises meaningfully from 4.1%, even a hawkish chair cuts — but he will be later than his predecessors would have been, and he will need the core measures below 2.75% first.

The most important thing about Warsh is not his hawkishness — it is his approach to communication, and the market has not fully priced what that means. Bond markets already have doubts: coverage of the 29 July meeting explicitly framed it as Warsh saying the Fed won't hesitate to stop inflation while the bond market expressed scepticism. That scepticism is the source of the term-premium problem visible in today's data. When a chair removes forward guidance, shortens the statement and withholds his own dot, he transfers uncertainty from the policy path to the yield curve. Investors who cannot read the reaction function from Fed communications must instead demand compensation for not knowing it — and that compensation shows up as term premium in long bonds. Today's session is the mechanism in action: the 30-year at 5.2593% and the 10-year at 4.6922% backing up 4.7-5.0bp while the 2-year moved only 2.67bp, widening 2s10s to a computed 52.31bps. The front end is anchored by a Fed that is clearly on hold; the long end is unanchored by a Fed that has stopped explaining itself, and it is being asked to fund a 5.99%-of-GDP deficit at the same time. The practical consequence for positioning is that the standard 'bad news is good news' reflex should be dialled down. Under a chair with a Bernanke-Yellen-Powell reaction function, a -23k payroll print buys you a rate cut and a bond rally. Under Warsh, with Core PCE at 3.29% and gasoline at $4.01, it buys you a slower economy and no cut — which is bad for credit at the 4th percentile and bad for equity multiples at a CAPE near 41x, without the offsetting duration rally.
