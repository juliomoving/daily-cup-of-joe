The biggest story of the session was not the size of the drawdown but its shape. The S&P 500 closed at 7,691.76, down 53.30 points or 0.69% from Monday's 7,745.06, yet the Nasdaq Composite fell nearly twice as hard at -1.33% (26,289.71) and the Russell 2000 dropped -1.30%. The Dow, carried by energy and healthcare, lost only 0.22%. That dispersion — a 1.11-percentage-point gap between the Dow and the Nasdaq in a single session (computed) — is the tell: this was a de-rating of duration-heavy growth, not a broad risk liquidation. Overseas the damage was worse where duration and yen funding meet: the Nikkei 225 shed 1,759.52 points (-2.54%) as the 10-year JGB rose 3.9bp to 2.96%, the highest-conviction move in global rates today. Euro Stoxx 50 fell 0.95%, the STOXX 600 0.69%, while the FTSE 100 eked out +0.07% on its energy weighting. The VIX rose 4.28% to 15.84 — up, but from a complacent base, and still a level that prices roughly a 1% daily move. Versus the prior session, the sequencing matters: US Treasuries rallied (10Y -1.8bp to 4.7041%, 30Y -2.3bp to 5.2826%, 2Y -0.4bp to 4.1709%) while Bunds (+3.8bp to 3.259%), JGBs (+3.9bp) and Gilts (+2.0bp to 5.08%) all sold off. A US equity selloff that pulls US yields down and pushes foreign yields up is a domestic-growth-scare signature layered on a foreign fiscal-supply signature — two different stories wearing the same clothes. Sentiment is best described as defensively rotational rather than fearful. Energy (+1.79% on the day, +4.20% on the week, +38.57% YTD), Health Care (+1.59%), Consumer Staples (+1.06%) and Financials (+0.45%) all rose while Info Tech fell 1.93% and Industrials 1.46%. Crude firmed — WTI $84.94 (+0.52%), Brent $91.30 (+0.47%) — with the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed since late February and the 60-day US-Iran negotiating window having expired on 17 August without a deal. Copper's -1.87% against natural gas's +3.20% says the same thing: energy scarcity, not industrial demand. And credit simply did not participate. US IG OAS ended at 0.80% (+1bp) and US HY at 2.68% (+2bp), both sitting in the 6th and 5th percentiles of their own history (computed) — 0% of the way to any of the four crisis benchmarks. When equities wobble 0.7% and high yield widens 2bp, either credit is asleep or equities are wrong. History says the answer arrives late, and never gently.

That divergence is worth more than either leg alone: US duration is being bought as a growth hedge while European, Japanese and UK duration is being sold on supply and policy normalisation. A market that bids crude and sells copper on the same day is pricing a supply shock, not a demand upswing. The US macro backdrop underneath all of this is genuinely awkward: nonfarm payrolls printed at -23k, unemployment at 4.1%, JOLTS openings at 7,359k and initial claims at a benign 209k, against a federal budget balance of -5.991% of GDP. A shrinking payroll with a 6%-of-GDP deficit and a 5.28% 30-year yield is a policy box with no comfortable exit.

Here are the levels that matter. US HY sits 35bp above its all-time tight of 2.33% and 1,703bp below its historic wide of 19.71% (computed). US IG sits 9bp above its 0.71% record tight, with a 6.18% historic wide overhead. EM USD is 21bp from its 1.39% record tight against a 10.87% peak. That distribution is the whole argument: the upside from here is measured in basis points and the downside in hundreds of them. What conditions would actually move these? To grind to new all-time tights (HY 2.33%, IG 0.71%) you need three things simultaneously — crude stabilising or falling, payrolls turning positive again after the -23k print, and Warsh holding rates without a hawkish surprise at Jackson Hole. That is a plausible path, and it earns you roughly 35bp of HY carry compression. To widen meaningfully you need only one thing to break. My markers: HY at 3.25% (roughly the level implied by a normalisation back toward the 25th percentile) would signal the complacency trade is over; HY at 4.00% with IG through 1.10% would signal a genuine credit cycle turn. Realistic triggers are a physical Hormuz escalation that puts Brent above $110, a second consecutive negative payroll print, or a long-end funding accident — the 30Y at 5.2826% and a -5.991%-of-GDP deficit are not a stable combination. The honest summary: credit is not mispriced because default risk is low; it is mispriced because there is no compensation left for the possibility of being wrong.

The commodities-versus-equities ratio at 0.02, at 200% of the 2000 condition, is the tell for how one-sided that capital allocation has become — and today's session, with Energy +1.79% against Info Tech -1.93%, is what the first inch of that reversal looks like. My verdict: not March 2000, but not 1997 either. Call it late 1999 in the equity market and late 2006 in the credit market, running simultaneously.
