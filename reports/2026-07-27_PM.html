The index screens were dull and the tape underneath was not. The S&P 500 closed 7,413.18, up all of 1.20 points (+0.02%), while the Nasdaq Composite fell 43.74 to 24,932.08 (-0.18%) and the Dow added 262.83 to 52,210.08 (+0.51%). The Russell 2000 outperformed at +0.62%. That mix — Dow and small caps up, Nasdaq down — is a rotation day, not a risk day, and VIX barely registered it at 18.67 (+0.09). The real story was crude. Brent printed 87.99 against a prior 96.78, a 9.08% session decline, with WTI marked 82.61 and a computed change of -7.50%. Natural gas fell 3.62%. That is an oil market pricing out war premium, and it lines up with the first two-week gap in announced US strikes on Iran (CNN, 25 July). Energy was the worst US sector on the day at -2.01%, even though it remains the best year-to-date at +27.68%. Rates rallied everywhere: US 10-year to 4.6487% (-2.84bp), 2-year 4.3223% (-0.83bp), 30-year 5.1365% (-2.09bp), Bund -3.9bp, Gilt -3.5bp, JGB -3.0bp — the classic lower-oil, lower-breakeven trade. Credit did not confirm the calm: US HY OAS widened 0.04 to 2.80% and EUR HY 0.04 to 2.85%, small moves off historically tight levels. FX was inert, DXY 101.497 (+0.03%), EUR/USD 1.1372, USD/JPY 163.72. Versus the prior session the sentiment shift is subtle but real — money moved from the AI complex and from energy into financials, staples and healthcare ahead of a genuinely heavy week: FOMC Wednesday under a new chair, Q2 GDP and the PCE deflator Thursday, Apple Thursday night, ECI Friday.

📊Markets

Breadth beat beta. The Dow's +262.83 (+0.51%) and the Russell 2000's +0.62% against the Nasdaq's -0.18% says the bid was in banks, insurers, staples and domestic cyclicals rather than mega-cap tech. Europe was flat to firm — Euro Stoxx 50 +0.02% at 6,282.21, STOXX 600 +0.02% at 644.62, FTSE 100 +0.42% at 10,781.75 on its energy-heavy index absorbing the crude hit better than expected. Nikkei closed +0.50% at 64,931.19. The bond market did the heavy lifting: a 2.84bp fall in the 10-year to 4.6487% with the 2-year down only 0.83bp is a bull-flattening driven by the inflation leg of the curve, exactly what a 9% Brent decline should produce. The 2s10s slope sits at 32.64bp per the computed set — positively sloped but historically flat. Credit's small widening (HY +0.04 to 2.80%, EM +0.02 to 1.66%) and VIX's +0.09 tick are the only hints of hesitation, and both are noise-level. Interpretation: this was a positioning day into an event-heavy week, with a large, genuine commodity repricing underneath a nearly unchanged headline index.

🔥Is Inflation Persistent?

Persistence score: 64/100 — Sticky, broad, and not beaten. The headline core measures look almost benign — Core CPI 2.6%, Median CPI 2.71%, 16% trimmed-mean CPI 2.63%, trimmed-mean PCE 2.41% — but Core PCE YoY at 3.41% is the number that matters to this Fed and it is a full point above target. The computed core-trend work (rendered separately) shows every underlying measure sitting in the upper third of its own history, with trimmed-mean PCE rising and the sticky components only grudgingly falling. The breadth data is the damning part: on the Dallas Fed distribution, 14.0% of the PCE basket is inflating faster than 10%, 10.0% at 5-10% and 34.0% at 3-5% — that is 58% of consumer spending rising at 3% or more, against just 9.6% in the 0-2% zone and 5.2% at 2-3%. Only 27.2% of the basket is outright falling. You do not get to 2% core PCE with 58% of the basket running above 3%; you get there by a small number of categories collapsing, which is what the 5.06% Flexible CPI (rising) versus 2.84% Sticky CPI (falling) split is telling you — the volatile stuff is re-accelerating while the persistent stuff drifts down slowly. Market pricing disagrees with the data: 5-year breakevens at 2.19% and 1-year at 1.81% say investors expect this resolved, and today's 9% oil decline supports that view for the next two quarters. My read is that headline inflation falls on energy through Q4 while core stays in the high-2s to low-3s, which is the worst combination for anyone hoping for cuts — it lets the Fed stay hawkish without paying a political price. Labour is not doing the disinflation work either: unemployment 4.2%, initial claims 187k, JOLTS openings 7,594k and average hourly earnings +3.5% describe a cooling but not cracking market. The one dovish datapoint is ECI at 0.9% YoY, and it is worth watching Friday's release closely.

Roughly two-thirds of the way to March 2000 on valuation, nowhere near it on sentiment, and past it on complacency. Break it down by dimension. On Equity Froth the computed set puts the Nasdaq's trailing P/E of 123.16x at 68% of the 175x it reached at the 2000 peak, and semiconductor valuation (SOX at 51.86x) at 80% of its peak condition — the chip complex is the frothiest single thing in this market. But price stretch is almost absent: the Nasdaq is only 4.1% above its 200-day average versus roughly 50% at the peak, an 8% reading. That combination — expensive but not extended — is the signature of a market that has already had its air-pocket in the momentum names, which the -5.47% one-week move in Communication Services confirms. On Late-cycle Catalysts, credit is where the reading blows past 2000: IG spreads at 0.79% register 134% of the 2000-peak tightness and the Baa-10Y spread at 161bp registers 149%. Credit investors are more complacent today than they were at the dot-com peak. The yield curve at +32.64bp is at 68% of its peak-condition reading, and both ISM readings (Manufacturing 53.3, Services 54.0) are expanding rather than rolling over, at 6% and 18% respectively — the real economy is simply not late-cycle in the way 2000 was. On Macro Regime, the dollar at 101.5 is 38% of the way to the 120 peak level, while the commodities-versus-equities ratio at 0.02 registers 200% — capital is more crowded into equities relative to real assets than it was in 2000, which is the most bubble-like number in the entire set. On Bear-Market Signposts the picture splits hard. VIX at 18.7 registers 164% of the peak-complacency condition and financial conditions (NFCI -0.55) 162% — volatility is suppressed and money is loose. But sentiment is the opposite of 2000: the AAII bull-bear spread is MINUS 12.7 points (18% of the peak reading), consumer confidence is 91.2 versus 140.8 (30%), and put/call at 0.79 registers only 33%. Retail investors are net bearish. That single fact is why this is not March 2000. Margin debt growing 41.2% year-on-year (57% of the peak's 72.7%) and SLOOS net tightening at 8.1% (89%) are the two that keep me from calling it benign. Overall: call it 60-65% of the way there, with the composition inverted — 2000 was euphoric sentiment on expensive stocks with tight credit; 2026 is sour sentiment on expensive stocks with tighter credit than 2000 ever saw. The bubble, if there is one, is in credit and in the assumption that AI earnings compound forever, not in retail exuberance. For scale on what a full unwind looks like, the reference drawdown ran from 5,048.62 on 10-Mar-2000 to 1,114.11 on 09-Oct-2002 — a 78% fall over 31 months.

🏦Fed Chair Warsh
NEAR TERM

Hold at Wednesday's 28-29 July meeting, with high confidence. I would put it at 90% no change, 8% hawkish hold with dissents pushing for a hike, 2% cut — those probabilities are my analyst estimate. The evidence is on the record: at the ECB's Sintra forum on 1 July, Warsh declined to hint at the July decision but said inflation is 'too high'; on 14 July, testifying on Capitol Hill after the CPI release, he said the latest improvement is not 'mission accomplished'. Crucially, the June Summary of Economic Projections dot plot showed all but one participant expecting rates to stay where they are or rise by end-2026. Even if Warsh personally wanted to move, the committee is not there. Watch the statement's length and the dissent count rather than the rate itself — he has already shortened the statement materially and stripped out forward guidance.

~1 YEAR

Between zero and two cuts by mid-2027, skewed to the low end, with a small but genuine tail for a hike. My analyst estimate: 45% zero cuts, 35% one to two cuts, 12% three or more, 8% a hike. This is where the inference starts, and I want to flag it clearly. The public record shows two Warsh personas that are hard to reconcile. As a private commentator and potential nominee, he argued for rate cuts paired with aggressive balance-sheet reduction — a 'regime change' framing in which the Fed shrinks its footprint dramatically and can therefore afford a lower policy rate. As chair, his on-record statements have been unambiguously hawkish on the price level: at Sintra he said that anyone expecting the Fed to accept inflation above 2% 'would be disappointed' and that 'we're going to deliver price stability'. My reading is that the cuts-plus-QT trade is still his intended destination, but he needs core inflation materially lower before he can sell it to a committee whose dots show no cuts. Core PCE at 3.41% does not permit it. The path I would underwrite: hold through 2026, balance-sheet runoff accelerating as the quieter lever he can pull without committee drama, and the first cut only once core PCE has a 2-handle — which on current breadth data (58% of the basket above 3%) is a 2027 story. The wildcard is credibility: he has emphasised political independence explicitly, which suggests he will resist White House pressure for cuts even at the cost of growth.

Warsh's first two months have been about resetting the Fed's communication contract, not its policy rate. The June statement was markedly shorter than its predecessors and led with the committee's pledge to 'deliver price stability' — a deliberate move away from Powell-era data-dependent hedging toward a single, declarative objective. CNN's 1 July piece framed his reform agenda as already winning support internationally. For markets, the practical implication is that forward guidance is being withdrawn as a tool, which mechanically raises the volatility of individual meetings: with no dot-plot hand-holding between meetings, each print does more work. Note also what he said about AI — that officials have become 'more open-minded' about AI's deflationary implications while still concluding that 'prices are too high'. That is an important nuance: he is not going to let an AI-productivity narrative justify tolerating a 3.41% core PCE. For Wednesday, the market has essentially nothing priced for a move, so the risk is entirely in the tone. A statement that hardens the language, or a press conference in which he links balance-sheet runoff to the inflation objective, is a hawkish surprise the front end has not discounted — and with the 2-year at 4.3223% and 1-year breakevens at just 1.81%, the front end is the vulnerable part of the curve.
